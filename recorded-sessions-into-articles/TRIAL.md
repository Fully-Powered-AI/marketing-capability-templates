# Recorded sessions into articles: one trial on its invented example

- **Product:** Claude
- **Model:** Claude Fable 5.2
- **Date:** 2026-11-09
- **Runs:** two. The first found gaps in the instructions; they were tightened, and the run recorded here was made on the tightened instructions the same day.

The inputs are the invented example published with this template. Brindlemoor Training, its trainer, the workshop, the participants and every word in the transcripts are invented, and no client data was used. This is a record of the second of two runs on that example. It is not a test in anyone's working environment.

## Inputs used

Exactly what the content coordinator supplies in [the example](example.md), and nothing else:

- the settings sheet for Brindlemoor Training, which also carries the privacy list and the confirmation that participants are told of the recording, as the template allows;
- three recordings with transcripts: "Meetings That Decide, week 3" (6 October 2026), "Brindlemoor planning call" (8 October 2026) and "Meetings That Decide, week 4" (13 October 2026);
- the dates to cover, 1 to 16 October 2026, the day of the run in the example, 16 October 2026, and the role of the person running it, the content coordinator.

The template's instructions for an AI assistant and its output format were given to the assistant as written, followed by these inputs. The example keeps its inputs and its published output on one page, and this was the second run, so both the published output and the first run's output had been seen before it. The run was made from the inputs, step by step, and compared with the published output afterwards.

The question it answers: why do my meetings end without a decision

In the second of two workshop sessions on this, a participant reported
back on the exercise from the week before. They had tried to write their
meeting's decision as one sentence, and the team argued about the sentence
for twenty minutes. Odalys Venn's answer: "That argument was the meeting."

The principle is a line she gave in both sessions: "Name the decision
before you open the discussion." The thing she sees go wrong in almost
every meeting she sits in on is that nobody knows what kind of meeting they
are in.

Four more of her lines, in the order she taught them:

1. "If you cannot say what is being decided, you are not ready to meet."
   Write the decision down before you send the invitation. If it will not
   come, do not hold the meeting yet.
2. "One decision, one owner." Choose the owner before the meeting: the
   person who will be asked about the decision in a month, who may not be
   the most senior person in the room.
3. "Write the decision down while everyone is still in the room." Then read
   it out loud. Her reason: "If two people hear it differently, you have
   not decided yet."
4. "A decision without a date is a wish." Before the meeting ends, say who
   does what, and by when.

The mistake the room made was to skip the first step. In the second session
she repeated the line because half the room said they had not done it. The
one who did do it took the argument for a failure. She took it for the
point: the team found out in one meeting what would otherwise have come
out slowly, by email.

This applies to a meeting that has something to decide. A participant asked
what to do when the point is only to share updates. Her answer: "Then it is
not a decision meeting, and you say so." Send the update in writing and
give people the half hour back.

Try it. Take a real meeting from your calendar this week and write its
decision as one sentence. If you cannot, cross the meeting out. Then
practise on something small with your own team: a five-minute meeting to
decide where you have lunch on Friday. Name the decision, ask for
objections, write it down, date it.

## What was observed

**What worked**

- Every part of the output format was produced: the review sheet first with all its sections, one article with every part from the opening moment to the closing invitation, the sources, and the list to check before publishing.
- Each of the eight quotations was searched for in the transcripts by a short script and found, unchanged, inside a single line labelled "Odalys". None is joined from two lines. The one that appears in both sessions is listed with both dates.
- The article gives five key lines word for word, the principle and four more, inside the three to six the instructions ask for.
- The planning call was set aside on its title and nothing was taken from it.
- The line from the unknown speaker was not quoted or used. The participant's name that Odalys speaks in one of her own lines, the employer a participant mentions, that participant's account of their workplace and the remark about a participant's health appear nowhere in the output. A search of the article for each of them found nothing.
- The word count, 398 from the opening to the closing invitation, is inside the 300 to 450 the settings ask for. None of the three never-used words appears. The closing invitation and its address match the settings.
- Nothing in the instructions called for a stop, and nothing was published, posted or sent.

**What the first run found, and what changed**

The first run, on the instructions as first written, produced the same subject, the same "[check]" line and an article of 415 words with ten quotations. It showed six places where the instruction block left the assistant to guess. Each now has a line in the block, and this run followed them:

- **Where one subject ends.** A subject is now one question a reader would ask. Naming the decision, giving it an owner, writing it down and dating it all answer "why do our meetings end without a decision", so they are one subject, as the first run had guessed.
- **Passing mentions.** Every subject not drafted is now listed under "Taught but not written", passing mentions included.
- **Article length and existing articles.** The block now says to keep to the length in the settings and to list, not draft, a subject an existing article already covers.
- **Existing articles and claims left out.** The output format now has a Coverage line for each. Both were filled here: no subject repeats the existing article, and no claim was left out.
- **"To check" and the word count.** "To check" is the number of "[check]" lines. Words are counted from the opening to the closing invitation.
- **Prepared on, and by whom.** The person running it states the date and their role, or the assistant asks once.

**Differences from the published example**

- The same subject was chosen and the same line was flagged with "[check]", for the same reason. Both take the same reading of it in the exercise. The title, the opening and the selection of lines differ: this run opens on the participant's report of the twenty-minute argument, where the example opens on what Odalys said at the start of the first session.
- Both quote eight lines, and seven are the same. This run quotes "Then it is not a decision meeting, and you say so." where the example quotes "An update is a broadcast."
- This run lists 4 subjects taught but not written; the example lists 5, with the count to seven as its own passing mention. Here it sits inside the objections subject, because it answers the same question.

**Where the instructions still leave room to guess**

- **What a participant said that is not a question.** The privacy list allows a question to be reported as "a participant asked". The article also reports, without quoting, what a participant said happened in their team. The instructions ask for the mistake the room made with nobody identifiable, so this was kept, but the rule for a participant's statement is not spelled out.
- **What counts as a result.** The instructions say to state no result outside the cleared statements. Odalys says a team "found out in twenty minutes what would have taken you three weeks to find out by email". The first run quoted the line and flagged it. This run paraphrased it without the figures. Whether an expert's remark about what happened in a participant's team is a result is left to judgment.
- **What is one detail.** The instructions now say to count each distinct name or detail once. A name, an employer and a health remark are clear. A participant's description of their own workplace meeting was counted as one detail; someone else might count it as two.
- **Where one question ends.** "One question a reader would ask" settled this example. Two people could still draw the line between "ending in a decision" and "finding out whether people agree" differently, and the share of the expert's lines moves with it.

**Shortcomings of this trial**

- Two runs by the same assistant, on one small invented example with two short transcripts. The second run had seen the first. It says nothing about long transcripts, captions with poor speaker labels, or notes in place of a transcript.
- The stop conditions were not exercised: the recording notice was confirmed, the expert's label was given, and sessions fell inside the dates.
- The claims rule and the existing-articles rule had nothing to act on: no claim needed leaving out, and no subject repeated the existing article.
- Nobody with the expert's knowledge reviewed the draft, so the reviewer checklist items that belong to the expert were not answered. The three that belong to the person who ran it (each quotation inside one expert line; no listed name, employer or client; the invitation matches the settings) were checked and hold.

## Edits after the recorded run

9 November 2026. The template was edited after the run recorded above, following its editorial review. It was not run again, so the output above came from the instructions as they stood before these edits. What changed in the instruction block:

- **The dates to cover.** If none are given, the block now gives the default the procedure already stated: the last [30] days, or the day after the previous run ended, said on the review sheet.
- **The "Partial:" line.** The block now says when to add it: a notes-only session, a blank setting, or a check that could not be made. The output format already carried the line.
- **A participant's statement that is not a question.** What a participant said about their own attempt at the exercise may be paraphrased as the mistake the room made, only when nothing in it identifies them. This closes the first point under "Where the instructions still leave room to guess".
- **What counts as a result.** The expert's remark on how a participant's attempt at the exercise went is teaching and may be paraphrased; a claim about what the expert's work earns, saves or cures is a result wherever it is said. This closes the second point.
- **Three to six key lines.** The limit counts the numbered key lines only, not a quotation elsewhere in the article.

The output format was not changed. In the example, the review sheet's summary now calls the share of lines an estimate, and the invented employer a participant mentions was renamed so that it does not resemble a company in another template's example; the name appears in the transcript only and in none of the output.
